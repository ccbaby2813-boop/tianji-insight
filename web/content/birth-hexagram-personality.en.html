Your Birth Hexagram: A 4,000-Year-Old Personality System

Long before MBTI asked you 93 questions, an older system asked only one: when were you born? The answer, converted into a six-line hexagram, has served as a personality mirror for four millennia.

The birth hexagram (本命卦) is the I Ching’s personality archetype, derived from the year, month, day and hour of your birth. Here is how it works — and why it still holds up next to modern psychometrics.

What is a birth hexagram?

In the I Ching tradition, your birth hexagram (本命卦, “original destiny hexagram”) is derived from the year, month, day and hour of your birth. Each element maps to a number, the numbers map to trigrams, and two trigrams stack into a hexagram — one of 64 possible life archetypes.

Think of it as a coordinate system for temperament: the lower trigram is your inner nature, the upper trigram is how the world sees you, and the moving lines mark the chapters where your life energy peaks and turns.

How to find yours

The classic formula (used by Shao Yong in the Song Dynasty):

  1. Take the year, month, day and hour of your birth as four numbers.
  2. Convert the lunar calendar into the “heavenly stems and earthly branches” values.
  3. Add the first two values and divide by 8: the remainder gives the upper trigram.
  4. Add all values and divide by 8: the remainder gives the lower trigram.
  5. Divide the total by 6: the remainder points to your moving line.

The eight trigram personalities

  • Qian (乾) — the Creative: decisive, driven, natural leadership
  • Kun (坤) — the Receptive: nurturing, adaptable, grounded
  • Zhen (震) — the Arousing: energetic, action-first, restless
  • Xun (巽) — the Gentle: penetrating, diplomatic, detail-minded
  • Kan (坎) — the Abysmal: cautious, deep, resilient under pressure
  • Li (离) — the Clinging: radiant, perceptive, relationship-driven
  • Gen (艮) — the Still: patient, steady, protective
  • Dui (兑) — the Joyous: expressive, social, pleasure-seeking

What your hexagram says about you

Stacking two trigrams creates nuance that single-letter MBTI types rarely capture. Example: the hexagram Qian-over-Qian (pure creativity) is the classic entrepreneur archetype — ambitious, self-reliant, allergic to micromanagement. Qian-over-Kun, by contrast, tempers that drive with receptivity: a leader who builds through people, not over them.

The moving line adds a time dimension: it reveals which life phase asks the most of you, and which of your qualities will surface under pressure. That “when” is something static personality tests simply do not model.

Modern parallels: MBTI, enneagram, and AI

Psychometric tools cluster people into types because human temperament is patterned, not random. The hexagram system does the same with far fewer inputs — and it adds two things modern tests lack: a relationship layer (which hexagrams naturally complement each other) and a time layer (how your energy shifts by season and life stage).

None of this claims to be science in the lab-test sense. It is a 4,000-year-old heuristic — a lens. Used with curiosity rather than fatalism, it is surprisingly good at naming patterns you already feel but have not articulated.
